# Introduction Writing

## Purpose

Use this reference to design or revise a biomedical Introduction as a convergent scientific argument. The Introduction should make the reader accept a sequence of linked decisions, not merely review a field or list the analyses performed.

## Central principle

Move from the broad problem to one precise unresolved relationship:

```text
disease or biological problem
-> decisive stage, subgroup, or context
-> central factor or exposure
-> central phenotype, compartment, or process
-> biological reason they belong together
-> exact knowledge gap
-> evidence layer needed to address the gap
-> study question and proportional objective
```

The final gap should be a relationship the study can actually test, not a broad claim that merely makes the field sound incomplete.

## Dual-line convergence

Use a dual-line structure when a paper joins two bodies of evidence, such as a circulating factor and an imaging phenotype, a cell state and a clinical outcome, or a molecular exposure and an organ compartment.

Build each line independently before joining them:

```text
Line A: biological identity -> prior disease evidence -> unresolved relevance
Line B: biological function -> prior phenotype evidence -> unresolved value
Bridge: shared process or interface -> precise A-B gap
```

Do not place two variables together only because both are available in the dataset. State the biological bridge that makes their relationship worth testing.

## Paragraph architecture

Choose the smallest structure that fits the study. A simple mechanistic paper may use three paragraphs; a multimodal or dual-line paper often needs four or five.

### Paragraph 1: define the decision-relevant problem

- Establish the disease or biological process briefly.
- Narrow to the stage, subgroup, transition, or context where uncertainty matters most.
- Explain the heterogeneity or unmet need that motivates the study.
- End by naming the type of biological signal or explanation that is needed.

Avoid opening with prevalence alone. Importance should lead to a specific scientific problem.

### Paragraph 2: establish the central factor

- Define the factor only to the depth needed for the paper.
- Select prior evidence that supports its biological and clinical relevance.
- Move from general association toward the exact disease stage or process studied here.
- End with the unresolved role that will connect to the next paragraph.

Do not catalog every known function or study. Each citation must advance the argument.

### Paragraph 3: establish the phenotype or biological compartment

- Briefly state what conventional research has emphasized.
- Explain why the focal phenotype or compartment adds a distinct layer.
- Give the biological functions that make it relevant to the central factor.
- Bring the two evidence lines together and state the exact relationship that remains unknown.

If this paragraph must carry both a large literature base and the bridge, split it rather than burying the gap.

### Paragraph 4: introduce the complementary evidence layer

- State what the primary measurement can establish.
- Name the biological layer it cannot resolve.
- Introduce the complementary modality only because it addresses that limitation.
- Define its role accurately: mechanistic testing, orthogonal validation, tissue context, spatial context, or hypothesis generation.

Use the pattern `current evidence can answer X, but not Y; modality Z addresses Y`. Do not justify multi-omics by technological breadth alone.

### Final paragraph: state the scientific question

- Open with the central question or hypothesis.
- Name the major evidence layers and the distinct question assigned to each.
- Close with the scientific objective or implication supported by the design.

Prefer a compact synthesis over `First... We then... Next... Finally...`. The final paragraph should explain why the study was assembled, not narrate the execution order.

## Paragraph-level construction

Within each paragraph, use this progression:

```text
established knowledge -> selected evidence -> narrowing turn -> unresolved point
```

The last sentence of one paragraph should create the need for the next. Useful transitions perform logical work: they narrow scope, expose a limitation, connect two evidence lines, or identify the remaining gap. Do not add transition words to sentences that lack a real logical turn.

## Method-introduction rule

Never introduce a platform merely because it was used. Establish the missing resolution first:

- imaging identifies a structural phenotype but not its cellular basis;
- bulk tissue detects an average signal but not its cellular source;
- observational data identify association but not direction or function;
- an animal model tests biology but does not by itself establish human relevance.

Then name the modality that addresses that specific layer. One modality should have one stated argumentative responsibility.

## Scope and claim control

- Do not imply causality in the Introduction when the design tests association.
- Do not present exploratory or independent datasets as direct validation when samples, exposures, or diagnoses are not linked.
- State the positive role of each evidence layer. Reserve extended defensive explanations of what it cannot prove for Methods or Discussion unless the boundary is essential to understanding the design.
- Do not preview every result. The Introduction should establish the question and strategy, not reproduce the abstract.

## Diagnostic checklist

Before finalizing the Introduction, verify:

1. Can the central unresolved relationship be written in one sentence?
2. Is the decisive stage, subgroup, or context named early?
3. Does every paragraph have one clear argumentative function?
4. Does each central variable have enough independent rationale?
5. Is there an explicit biological bridge between the variables?
6. Does every method answer a previously stated limitation?
7. Does the final paragraph synthesize the study rather than list workflow steps?
8. Are repeated claims, defensive caveats, and background details that do not advance the gap removed?

## Common failures

- Broad disease importance without a decision-relevant stage or context.
- Two well-described variables with no biological reason to study them together.
- A long paragraph carrying the conventional field, the new phenotype, the mechanism, and the gap at once.
- Repeating the same `tissue-level context` or `hypothesis-generating` justification in adjacent paragraphs.
- Introducing single-cell, spatial, or multi-omics data as a display of technical breadth.
- Ending with a project-report sequence of analyses instead of one central question.
