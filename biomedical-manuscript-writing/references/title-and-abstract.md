# Title and Abstract

## Purpose

Use this file when drafting or revising titles and abstracts with clear story and calibrated claims. Reference patterns should not be mechanically imitated.

## Title Rules

### Rule 1: Put the central axis in the title

The title usually centers on one of these:

- the regulator
- the cell population
- the niche interaction
- the origin claim
- the predictive axis

Bad pattern:

- broad disease statement plus method plus many pathways

Preferred pattern:

- one central actor plus one consequence in one biological context

### Rule 2: Use a strong verb only when the evidence earns it

Common corpus verbs and their usual force:

- `decodes`: useful when integrative analysis identifies a coherent driving program
- `directs`: stronger causal language for upstream control supported by function
- `orchestrates`: suited for distributed or multicellular regulation with multiple linked steps
- `predicts`: suited for biomarkers or response associations with supportive validation
- `regulates` or `controls`: broad mechanistic verbs when causality is supported but not absolute
- `replenishes` or `sustains`: good for stem-cell and lineage stories

### Rule 3: Mention the platform only if it is central

Good cases:

- atlas paper
- single-cell discovery paper
- integrative multi-omics prioritization paper

Otherwise:

- keep the biology in front and leave the platform for the abstract

## Title Families

### Family A: Mechanistic axis title

Template:

- `X-derived Y directs Z via A in B`
- `The X-dependent A/B axis predicts C in D`
- `X controls Y through Z in context`

Use when:

- one upstream signal or regulator clearly anchors the story

### Family B: Discovery-through-platform title

Template:

- `Integrative multi-omics analysis decodes X-driven Y in Z`
- `Decoding X at single-cell resolution`
- `Delineating X from a single-cell perspective`

Use when:

- the paper's novelty partly depends on the resolving power of the platform

### Family C: Cell population or lineage title

Template:

- `A quiescent cell population replenishes X to drive Y`
- `Glial origin of X in a model system`
- `Developmental plasticity of X in Y and Z`

Use when:

- the central answer is a cell identity, source, or developmental behavior

### Family D: Review title

Template:

- `Pleiotropic role and bidirectional immunomodulation of X in Y`
- `Advances in X in health and disease`
- `X-dependent divergence of Y in Z`

Use when:

- the review turns on heterogeneity, dual roles, or translational divergence

## Abstract Skeleton for Mechanistic Papers

Use this five-part order.

### Sentence block 1: Problem and gap

Do:

- define why the biological system matters
- make the unresolved point precise

Example shape:

- `X is important, but the mechanism driving Y remains unclear.`
- `Model Z is widely used, yet its translational relevance is incompletely defined.`

### Sentence block 2: Platform and prioritization

Do:

- say what integrated approach was used
- identify the prioritized regulator, cell population, or axis

Example shape:

- `Using a controlled model with single-cell transcriptomics, we identified ...`
- `Leveraging integrative multi-omics analysis, we prioritized ...`

### Sentence block 3: Core finding

Do:

- name the decisive biological entity early
- state the main phenotype or process it explains

### Sentence block 4: Mechanistic bridge

Do:

- use `Mechanistically,` only when you can actually bridge upstream to downstream
- keep to one main axis

### Sentence block 5: Implication

Do:

- end with therapeutic, regenerative, biomarker, or translational meaning
- keep the last sentence proportional to the evidence

## Abstract Skeleton for Atlas or Comparative Papers

Use this order:

1. define why the comparison matters
2. state the integration strategy
3. identify conserved patterns
4. identify key species-specific or condition-specific differences
5. state what is translatable and what is not

## Abstract Skeleton for Reviews

Use this order:

1. state field importance
2. identify complexity, heterogeneity, or contradiction
3. explain how the review is organized
4. highlight unresolved mechanisms or future directions
5. end with therapeutic or clinical relevance

## Preferred Sentence Habits

- Open strongly with the biological or clinical importance.
- Use `Here,` once if it sharpens the transition into the study.
- Use `Mechanistically,` sparingly and only with real cause-and-effect support.
- Reserve the final sentence for significance, not for repeating the result.

## Common Abstract Mistakes

### Mistake 1: Too many findings in parallel

Fix:

- choose the one finding that answers the main question and demote the rest

### Mistake 2: Methods overload

Fix:

- compress the methods into one phrase unless the platform is itself a key contribution

### Mistake 3: Jump from omics to therapy

Fix:

- insert the mechanistic and functional bridge before making translational claims
